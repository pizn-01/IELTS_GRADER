# From Feedback to Study Plan: Using Your IELTS AI Tutor Report

A detailed Writing report is only useful if it changes what you do next week. Many candidates collect scores, feel motivated for a day, then return to random practice. This guide from **IELTS AI Tutor by IELTSGRADER** shows how to turn feedback into a simple, repeatable study plan.

## The problem with “score collecting”

Opening a report and reading “Band 6.5” does not improve Coherence. Improvement comes from:

1. Identifying the bottleneck criterion  
2. Practising that skill deliberately  
3. Rewriting with the feedback in mind  
4. Retesting under timed conditions  

Your AI tutor report is the input. The study plan is the output.

## Step 1: Read the report in the right order

Ignore the overall band for five minutes. Look at:

| Order | Look at | Why |
|-------|---------|-----|
| 1 | Lowest criterion | This caps your average |
| 2 | Fix cards / sentence notes | These become drills |
| 3 | Second-lowest criterion | Backup focus if time allows |
| 4 | Overall band | Context only |

If Task Response is 6 and Grammar is 7.5, grammar drills are not your priority, even if error highlighting feels satisfying.

Refresh criteria meanings in [how IELTS writing is scored](/blog/how-ielts-writing-is-scored).

## Step 2: Translate scores into a weekly goal

Use one sentence:

> For the next 7 days, I will raise **[criterion]** by practising **[specific skill]** in **[number]** sessions.

Examples:

- Raise **Coherence** by practising topic sentences in 5 short paragraph sessions.  
- Raise **Task Response** by forcing example + explanation in every body paragraph across 3 essays.  
- Raise **Lexical Resource** by using a 20-collocation bank on one topic family.  
- Raise **Grammar** by logging and rewriting only article/agreement errors.

One goal beats five vague intentions.

## Step 3: Build a 7-day plan from fix cards

### Template

| Day | Activity | Time | Linked to fix card? |
|-----|----------|------|---------------------|
| 1 | Drill only (no full essay) | 30–40 min | Yes |
| 2 | Drill + one rewritten paragraph | 40 min | Yes |
| 3 | Timed Task 2 or Task 1 | 20–40 min | Partial |
| 4 | Rewrite yesterday’s weak paragraphs | 30 min | Yes |
| 5 | Second drill type for same criterion | 30 min | Yes |
| 6 | Light review / error log | 20 min | Yes |
| 7 | Timed task + new check | 40–60 min | Measure |

Submit Day 7 work to the [essay checker](/ielts-essay-checker) or [Task 2 checker](/ielts-task-2-checker) and compare the target criterion only.

### Example: Coherence was lowest

Borrow structure from the [14-day Coherence plan](/blog/coherence-cohesion-14-day-plan), compressed into 7 days: topic sentences, one-idea paragraphs, referencing, then a timed essay.

### Example: Task Response was lowest

Study [Band 6 vs 7](/blog/band-6-vs-7-task-2) examples; practise claim → explain → example; rewrite one thin body paragraph daily for four days.

## Step 4: Always schedule a rewrite

Feedback without rewrite is entertainment.

**Rewrite rules:**

- Same prompt within 48 hours for the first rewrite  
- Change structure/development, not only a few adjectives  
- Re-check if your plan includes enough credits/attempts  

Then write a **new** timed prompt so you transfer the skill beyond one essay.

## Step 5: Use mocks as checkpoints, not daily fuel

Full mocks are for measurement and timing. Daily work should be targeted.

Recommended rhythm:

- **Most days:** drills + short writing  
- **Once a week:** full or partial mock  
- **After each mock:** new 7-day plan  

Details: [mock writing practice guide](/blog/ielts-mock-writing-practice-guide) and [mock exam to 14-day sprint](/blog/mock-exam-to-14-day-sprint).

## Step 6: Track progress in a simple log

| Week | Target criterion | Start | End | Notes |
|------|------------------|-------|-----|-------|
| 1 | CC | 6.0 | 6.5 | Topic sentences improved; still rush endings |
| 2 | CC | 6.5 | 6.5 | Timing issue; plan longer outlines |
| 3 | TR | 6.0 | 6.5 | Examples more specific |

Flat weeks are data. They tell you to change the drill, not to panic. Plateau patterns: [stuck at 6.5](/blog/stuck-at-band-6-5-plateau).

## Sample plans by profile

### Profile A: Strong grammar, weak structure

- Priority: Coherence  
- Tools: paragraph job maps, [CC 14-day plan](/blog/coherence-cohesion-14-day-plan)  
- Avoid: advanced grammar YouTube rabbit holes  

### Profile B: Good ideas, repetitive vocabulary

- Priority: Lexical Resource  
- Tools: [trends vocabulary](/blog/task-1-trends-vocabulary) (Task 1), [LR Band 6→7](/blog/lexical-resource-band-6-to-7)  
- Avoid: thesaurus stuffing  

### Profile C: Misses parts of the question / bullets

- Priority: TR or TA  
- Tools: bullet ticks for GT ([letter checklist](/blog/gt-formal-letter-checklist)), overview-first for Academic Task 1, opinion consistency checks for Task 2  
- Avoid: writing longer introductions  

### Profile D: Everything around 6.5

- Priority: lowest criterion this week only  
- Tools: weekly mock + surgical drills  
- Read: [Tutor’s Verdict / plateau](/blog/stuck-at-band-6-5-plateau)  

## Turning one report into a 14-day sprint

Days 1–7: target criterion drills + one rewrite + one mid check.  
Days 8–14: maintain gains; add second criterion lightly; end with a [mock writing test](/ielts-mock-writing-test).

If you follow a product journey, [mock → report → sprint](/blog/mock-exam-to-14-day-sprint) describes the same arc end-to-end.

## Common planning mistakes

1. Trying to fix all four criteria in one week  
2. Doing only new essays, never rewrites  
3. Changing focus after every single check  
4. Ignoring Task 1/letters because Task 2 feels more important  
5. Confusing “reading model essays” with practice
