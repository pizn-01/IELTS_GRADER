## Task Response vs Task Achievement in one line

Task Response (TR) applies to Task 2 essays and asks whether you answered the question with a clear, developed position; Task Achievement (TA) applies to Task 1 charts and letters and asks whether you covered the required information accurately. They are sibling criteria with different jobs — for the full comparison, see [Task Response vs Task Achievement](/blog/task-response-vs-achievement). This guide focuses only on Task 2 TR repair.

## The four things Task Response actually checks

1. Did you address every part of the prompt?
2. Is your position clear and consistent (where the type requires one)?
3. Are your ideas developed with explanation and example, not just stated?
4. Does the essay stay relevant from introduction to conclusion?

Weak Task Response almost always traces back to one of these four, not to a lack of "interesting ideas." ## Drill 1: the "so what" test for every claim

After each topic sentence, ask "so what?" out loud. If you cannot answer with a reason and a concrete example in the next two sentences, the paragraph is still at claim-only depth.

**Before:**

> Public transport is important for cities. It helps a lot of people.

**After (passes the "so what" test):**

> Public transport is important for cities because it reduces the number of private cars competing for limited road space during peak hours. In cities that expanded metro coverage, for example, average commute times fell even as population grew, because fewer residents needed to drive to reach the same destinations.

Run this drill on your last three practice essays: underline every topic sentence, then check whether the next two sentences actually answer "so what."

## Drill 2: the coverage checklist

Before writing, list every distinct instruction in the prompt as a short checklist, then tick each one off as you plan. This catches the most common TR failure across every essay type: leaving part of the question unanswered.

| Essay type | Example checklist item easy to miss |
|------------|----------------------------------------|
| Discuss both views | Stating your own opinion, not just describing both views |
| Advantages/disadvantages (outweigh) | Actually stating which side wins, not just listing both |
| Problem/solution | Solutions that match the specific causes named, not generic advice |
| Two-part question | Giving the second question a full paragraph, not one sentence |

Full breakdowns of each type's specific coverage traps live in the [Task 2 essay types hub](/blog/task-2-essay-types-hub).

## Drill 3: the position-tracking read-through

Read your finished essay once, underlining only the sentences that state your position (introduction, any body-paragraph mini-links, and the conclusion). Then check: do all underlined sentences agree with each other? Position drift — starting "I agree" and finishing "it depends" — is a frequent, avoidable TR loss.

**Drifted (fails the read-through):**

> Introduction: "I largely agree that remote work benefits employees." … Conclusion: "Overall, it really depends on the person and the job."

**Consistent (passes the read-through):**

> Introduction: "I largely agree that remote work benefits employees." … Conclusion: "For these reasons, I maintain that remote work is largely beneficial, provided communication stays structured."

## Drill 4: the generic-example swap

Replace any example that could apply to literally any essay ("in many countries," "some people say") with one naming a specific, plausible scenario: a role, a place, a policy, or a situation. Generic examples signal to an examiner that an idea was never really developed, only gestured at.

**Generic:**

> For example, in many countries this is a common problem and people are affected.

**Specific:**

> For example, a nurse finishing a night shift in a city with unreliable late-night buses may have no practical way to get home safely without a private car.

Do this swap for at least one example per practice essay until naming a specific scenario becomes automatic under time pressure.

## Drill 5: the conclusion mirror check

Your conclusion should mirror your introduction's position in different words, with no new arguments and no softening. Read only your introduction and conclusion together, ignoring the body paragraphs — if they do not clearly agree, Task Response is likely being undercut by the ending.

**Fails the mirror check:**

> Introduction states a clear "largely agree" position. Conclusion introduces a brand-new argument about cost that was never discussed in the bodies, then ends on a vague "it could go either way."

**Passes the mirror check:**

> Introduction and conclusion both state the same extent, with the conclusion adding no new claims, only a compressed restatement of the two reasons already developed.

## Which drill to run first

If you only have time for one drill before an exam, choose based on what your last checked essay flagged most often:

| If your reports usually say… | Start with |
|-------------------------------|------------|
| "Ideas need more development" | Drill 1 (the "so what" test) |
| "Part of the question was not addressed" | Drill 2 (the coverage checklist) |
| "Position is unclear or inconsistent" | Drill 3 (position-tracking read-through) |
| "Examples are generic" | Drill 4 (the generic-example swap) |
| "Conclusion introduces new ideas" | Drill 5 (the conclusion mirror check) |

Running the one drill that matches your actual feedback pattern, repeatedly across several practice essays, moves Task Response faster than attempting all five drills once and moving on.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the fastest way to raise a low Task Response score?

Run the coverage checklist before writing so every part of the prompt is planned for, then apply the "so what" test to each topic sentence while drafting. Missing coverage and undeveloped claims are the two most common, most fixable causes.

### Is Task Response the same as Task Achievement?

No. Task Response applies to Task 2 essays; Task Achievement applies to Task 1 charts and letters. See [Task Response vs Task Achievement](/blog/task-response-vs-achievement) for the full distinction.

### Can strong vocabulary compensate for weak Task Response?

No. Task Response is scored independently of Lexical Resource. An essay with simple but accurate language and full coverage can outscore one with advanced vocabulary that misses part of the question, since coverage and development are judged on their own merits, not averaged against how the essay sounds.

### How do I know if my position is drifting?

Underline every sentence that states your position — introduction, body mini-links, and conclusion — and check they all agree. If the conclusion contradicts or softens the introduction, that is drift, and it usually happens when a writer runs out of time and rushes the final sentences without checking back against the opening stance.

### Do I need a "real" example, or can I invent one?

A plausible, specific invented scenario is fine and expected — IELTS is not testing factual accuracy, only whether the example is concrete and relevant enough to develop your point. Naming a role, a place, or a situation is usually enough to move an example from generic to developed.

## Next steps

Task Response improves fastest through repetition of specific drills, not general "write more" practice.
