# Two-Part Question Essay Structure IELTS Task 2

Two-part question prompts hide a simple but costly trap: candidates answer the first question thoroughly, then give the second question a single closing sentence. Task Response treats an under-answered second part as a major gap, no matter how strong the first half is. This structure from **IELTS AI Tutor by IELTSGRADER** gives each question its own full paragraph so nothing gets shortchanged.

## The unique practice prompt for this template

**Prompt:** These days, many people change their career two or three times during their working life, rather than staying in one profession. Why do people make this decision? Is this a positive or negative development?

Every worked paragraph below develops this career-change prompt, so the two questions are shown answered separately and in full on one coherent topic.

## How to recognise a two-part question

Two-part questions contain two distinct question marks, and unlike other Task 2 types, the two questions are often not linked by a shared keyword like "views" or "advantages." Typical patterns include:

- "Why does X happen? Is this a positive or negative trend?"
- "What are the reasons for X? What can be done about it?" (this variant overlaps with problem/solution — check whether "reasons" or "causes" language dominates)
- "How has X changed? What are the effects of this change?"

If you can underline two separate question marks with two separate topics attached, you are looking at this type, not agree/disagree or discuss-both-views.

## The 4-paragraph two-part map

| Paragraph | Job |
|-----------|-----|
| Introduction | Paraphrase both questions in one or two sentences |
| Body 1 | Answer question 1 fully, with a reason and example |
| Body 2 | Answer question 2 fully, with a reason and example |
| Conclusion | Short synthesis connecting both answers, if useful |

This follows the same 4-paragraph default used across Task 2 types — see the [full map](/blog/task-2-essay-types-hub#the-4-paragraph-default-map) for how other types adapt it.

## Giving both questions equal development

Equal development does not require identical sentence counts, but each body paragraph needs its own claim, explanation, and example — the same standard as any other Task 2 body paragraph. A common failure pattern gives question 1 four sentences of real content and question 2 a single generic line, which reads as an incomplete answer even if question 1 was excellent.

**Unequal (weaker):**

> Body 1 (why people change careers): four developed sentences with examples. Body 2 (is this positive or negative): "I think this is a positive development for society."

**Equal (stronger):**

> Body 2 gets the same treatment: a clear stance (positive/negative/mixed), a reason explaining why, and a concrete example illustrating that reason — matching body 1's depth.

## Mini sample: both body paragraphs

**Body 1 (why people change careers):**

> People increasingly change careers multiple times mainly because industries themselves are shifting faster than in previous generations, making a single skill set less reliable across an entire working life. A worker trained in traditional retail management, for example, may find that automation and online shopping have reduced demand for that exact role within a decade, prompting a deliberate move into a growing field such as digital marketing or logistics. Greater access to short online courses has also lowered the practical barrier to retraining compared with previous decades, when switching fields often required starting a degree from scratch.

**Body 2 (positive or negative development):**

> On balance, this trend is a positive development, both for individuals and for the wider economy. Workers who change careers deliberately, rather than being forced out with no alternative, often report higher long-term job satisfaction, since they can move toward roles that better match evolving interests or family circumstances. Economically, a workforce that retrains readily can also fill emerging skill shortages faster than one where employees remain fixed in declining industries, which reduces the mismatch between available jobs and available skills.

**Conclusion (short synthesis):**

> Career changes are therefore best understood as a rational response to a faster-changing job market, and one that, managed well, benefits both individuals and the wider economy.

## The missing-part Task Response trap

The most damaging version of this trap is not a short second paragraph — it is *no answer at all* to one question, often because a candidate misread the prompt as a single-question type and only planned one body paragraph. Before writing, count the question marks in the prompt and write down what each one is actually asking; if there are two, both need a dedicated body paragraph, not a shared one.

## Quick self-check before you submit

With about two minutes left, run through these checks:

1. **Count the question marks again** and confirm each one has its own body paragraph, not a shared sentence at the end of the other paragraph.
2. **Read your body 1 and body 2 topic sentences.** Each should clearly restate a different question from the prompt, not the same idea rephrased twice.
3. **Check word balance.** If one body paragraph is noticeably shorter than the other, the shorter one likely needs another sentence of explanation or example.
4. **Confirm your conclusion references both answers**, even briefly, rather than only the question you found easier to discuss.

## Two-part questions vs discuss-both-views

Both types can look similar at a glance because each has two "parts" to cover, but the relationship between the parts is different. Discuss-both-views presents two *competing opinions* about the same issue and asks you to weigh them before giving your own view. A two-part question asks two *separate, often unrelated* questions about one topic — there is no need to weigh one question against the other or to pick a side between them, only to answer each fully. Mixing the two approaches, such as trying to "give an opinion" on a two-part question that never asked for one, can waste development space that should go toward the actual second question.

## Try this yourself

**Prompt:** In many countries, more people are choosing to live alone rather than with family or a partner. Why is this happening? Do you think this is a positive or negative trend?

1. Underline both questions and write one sentence for each stating exactly what it asks.
2. Plan one body paragraph per question, each with a claim, explanation, and example.
3. Write the full essay in 40 minutes, then [have your essay checked free](/ielts-task-2-checker) and confirm the Task Response comment shows both questions were answered in full.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know if a prompt is a two-part question rather than another type?

Look for two separate question marks attached to two distinct topics, without a shared keyword like "views," "advantages," or "solutions" tying them together explicitly.

### Do both questions need equal length answers?

Not identical length, but equal development — each needs its own claim, explanation, and example, matching the standard of any strong Task 2 body paragraph.

### What happens if I only answer one part fully?

Task Response treats the missing or thin part as a major gap. A brilliantly answered first question cannot fully compensate for a one-sentence second answer.

### Should I use three paragraphs instead of two for the body?

Four paragraphs (one body per question) is usually enough. A third body paragraph can help only if one question genuinely splits into two distinct sub-strands that do not fit together.

### Is this the same as a discuss-both-views essay?

No. Discuss-both-views compares two opinions about one topic and asks for your view; a two-part question asks two separate, often unrelated questions about the same topic. See [discuss both views essay structure](/blog/discuss-both-views-structure) for the contrast.
