# Top Grammar Errors That Cap IELTS at 6.5

Most Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA) problems at Band 6.5 are not knowledge gaps — candidates can usually explain the rule when asked calmly. They are **time-pressure errors**: patterns that appear specifically when writing fast, planning a complex sentence mid-clause, or self-correcting halfway through a thought. This list from **IELTS AI Tutor by IELTSGRADER** targets exactly those ten patterns, not a generic ESL grammar review. ## Why these are timed-writing errors, not knowledge gaps

Under 40-minute exam pressure, writers commit to a complex sentence structure before finishing the thought, then patch the ending without checking whether the beginning still agrees. That mid-sentence patching is where most of these ten patterns originate — which is why a candidate who scores well on an untimed grammar quiz can still repeat the same errors in a timed script.

## 1–3: Sentence-collapse errors

### 1. Double conjunction ("Although… but…")

**Before:** Although online learning have many benefits, but students feel isolated.

**After:** Although online learning has many benefits, students often feel isolated.

Starting with "Although" already signals contrast; adding "but" is redundant and non-standard. This happens when a writer plans a contrast sentence, forgets the opening word already did the job, and adds a second contrast marker out of habit.

### 2. Comma splice in the topic sentence

**Before:** The government should invest in public transport, this would reduce traffic.

**After:** The government should invest in public transport, which would reduce traffic.

Two independent clauses joined only by a comma is a frequent topic-sentence error, because writers rush the first sentence of a paragraph to get to the "real" content faster.

### 3. Run-on conclusion sentence

**Before:** In conclusion technology has changed education it has made learning easier and more accessible for everyone around the world today.

**After:** In conclusion, technology has changed education, making learning easier and more accessible worldwide.

Conclusions written in the final rushed minute often lose punctuation discipline entirely, producing one long unpunctuated sentence.

## 4–6: Agreement and reference errors under pressure

### 4. Subject-verb agreement after a long subject

**Before:** The number of students who choose to study abroad every year are increasing.

**After:** The number of students who choose to study abroad every year is increasing.

By the time the verb arrives, the writer has mentally attached it to the nearest plural noun ("students") rather than the true subject ("the number"). This error rate rises sharply as subjects get longer.

### 5. Unclear pronoun reference

**Before:** Parents often disagree with teachers about homework, and this causes problems, which makes it worse.

**After:** Parents often disagree with teachers about homework, and this disagreement can strain the parent-teacher relationship.

"This" and "it" with no clear antecedent are a fast way to save time mid-sentence, but they blur meaning and are flagged under both GRA and Coherence.

### 6. Countable/uncountable mismatch under speed

**Before:** Governments should provide more informations and researches to the public.

**After:** Governments should provide more information and research to the public.

"Information" and "research" are uncountable in standard academic English; this slip appears more often in fast writing than in careful editing, because the plural "-s" is a fluent, automatic habit for most nouns.

## 7–8: Tense-timeline errors

### 7. Mixed tense in a single argument

**Before:** Fifty years ago, most people work in agriculture, but today it changes completely.

**After:** Fifty years ago, most people worked in agriculture, but today the situation has changed completely.

Writers often decide the timeline mid-sentence rather than before starting it, producing a present-tense verb attached to a clearly past time marker.

### 8. Overcorrected passive voice

**Before:** It is believed by many people that is caused by climate change.

**After:** Many people believe that this is caused by climate change.

Attempting a "sophisticated" passive construction mid-sentence, then losing track of the grammar needed to complete it, produces a broken clause that a simpler active sentence would have avoided entirely.

## 9–10: Article and preposition slips

### 9. Missing article with a unique reference

**Before:** Government should reduce pollution in environment.

**After:** The government should reduce pollution in the environment.

Fast writing frequently drops articles before nouns that are unique or previously established in context ("the government," "the environment"), especially for writers whose first language does not use articles the same way.

### 10. Wrong preposition in a fixed academic collocation

**Before:** This has a huge impact to people's lives and depends of many factors.

**After:** This has a huge impact on people's lives and depends on many factors.

Fixed collocations ("impact on," "depend on") are learned as whole chunks; under time pressure, writers default to a more literal or first-language-influenced preposition instead of the memorised chunk.

## How to drill these without a full grammar review

1. **Do not study all ten at once.** Pick the two patterns that appear most in your last three essays.
2. **Keep a personal error log**, not a generic textbook list — write your own before/after pairs, not invented examples.
3. **Rewrite only sentences containing your two target patterns** from a previous essay, timed, in five minutes.
4. **Re-check with a fresh timed essay** the following week to see whether the pattern rate actually fell.

This targeted approach fits the same logic as the GRA section of the [error taxonomy](/blog/ielts-writing-error-taxonomy#4-grammatical-range-accuracy-errors), which groups mistakes by family rather than treating every slip as equally urgent.

## Meaning-blocking vs cosmetic: which to fix first

Not all ten patterns carry equal weight. Comma splices in topic sentences, mixed tense across an entire argument, and broken passive attempts can genuinely confuse a reader about who did what and when — these are meaning-blocking and should be drilled first. Missing articles and minor preposition slips are usually cosmetic: an examiner can still follow the sentence perfectly even with the error present. A Band 6.5 script often has a mix of both; fixing the meaning-blocking patterns first tends to move GRA more than polishing cosmetic ones, even though cosmetic errors are often easier to notice and therefore easier to over-focus on.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do I make grammar mistakes in the exam that I never make in practice?

Time pressure forces you to commit to a sentence structure before finishing the thought, which is exactly when patterns like double conjunctions, tense mixing, and broken passives appear. Untimed accuracy does not always predict timed accuracy.

### Which of these ten errors matters most for Band 6.5?

Meaning-blocking patterns — comma splices in topic sentences, mixed tense across an argument, and broken passive attempts — tend to matter more than smaller slips like a missing article, because they can confuse who did what or when.

### Can I fix all ten patterns in one week?

Focus on two or three that actually appear in your own writing rather than all ten generically. A personal error log targeting your real patterns is far more effective than a broad grammar refresher.

### Does using more complex grammar always help my score?

No. Attempting complex structures you cannot yet control, like the overcorrected passive example above, often produces more errors than a simpler, accurate sentence would.
