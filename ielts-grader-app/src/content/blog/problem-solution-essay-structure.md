# Problem Solution Essay Structure for IELTS Task 2

Problem/solution prompts fail most often not because candidates lack ideas, but because the solutions do not connect to the problems named earlier in the essay. This structure from **IELTS AI Tutor by IELTSGRADER** gives each paragraph a specific job so causes and solutions stay matched, plus a check for the vague-solution trap that quietly caps Task Response.

## The unique practice prompt for this template

**Prompt:** Traffic congestion has become a serious problem in many major cities. What are the causes of this problem, and what measures could be taken to solve it?

Every worked paragraph in this guide develops this urban-traffic prompt so causes and solutions are shown matched to one specific, real topic.

## Causes vs problems: read the exact wording

Some prompts ask "what problems does X cause", which wants *effects* (for example, what traffic congestion causes: lost productivity, pollution, stress). Others ask "what are the causes of this problem", which wants the *reasons behind* the problem (for example, what causes traffic congestion: too many private cars, poor public transport, weak urban planning). Reading the exact verb — "causes" vs "problems caused by" — determines which direction body 1 should go, and mixing the two up is a Task Response error before a single solution is written.

## The 4-paragraph problem-solution map

| Paragraph | Job |
|-----------|-----|
| Introduction | Paraphrase the issue; preview that you will cover causes (or problems) and solutions |
| Body 1 | Explain one or two specific causes or problems, each with a brief example |
| Body 2 | Offer solutions that respond directly to the causes or problems named in body 1 |
| Conclusion | Briefly restate the link between the problem and your proposed solutions |

This follows the same 4-paragraph default used across Task 2 types — see the [full map](/blog/task-2-essay-types-hub#the-4-paragraph-default-map) for how it adapts to other question types.

## Matching solutions to problems, not general ideas

The strongest version of this essay type treats body 1 and body 2 as a matched pair: each cause named should have a corresponding fix in body 2, in roughly the same order. A body 2 that introduces entirely new ideas unrelated to body 1's causes reads as two separate mini-essays rather than one coherent argument.

**Unmatched (weaker):**

> Body 1 cause: too many private cars on the road. Body 2 solution: the government should improve education about pollution.

The solution addresses a different concern (environmental awareness) rather than the stated cause (car volume).

**Matched (stronger):**

> Body 1 cause: too many private cars on the road, driven partly by unreliable public transport. Body 2 solution: expanding and subsidising public transport directly reduces the incentive to drive, which responds to the exact cause named.

## Mini sample: both body paragraphs

**Body 1 (causes):**

> Traffic congestion in large cities usually stems from two compounding causes: rapid growth in private car ownership and public transport networks that have not expanded at the same pace. When a city's population grows faster than its rail or bus capacity, commuters who might otherwise choose public transport are pushed toward driving instead, even when they would prefer not to sit in traffic. Poor urban planning compounds this, since sprawling suburbs built around car access make walking or cycling impractical for daily commutes.

**Body 2 (matching solutions):**

> Addressing this therefore requires improving public transport capacity before, or alongside, any restriction on private cars. Extending metro and bus rapid transit lines to underserved suburbs gives commuters a genuine alternative rather than an inconvenient one, directly reducing the demand for private car trips created in body 1. Congestion pricing in city centres can further discourage unnecessary car use, provided the revenue is reinvested into the public transport expansion that makes the alternative realistic in the first place.

## The vague-solutions trap

Vague solutions restate the problem in optimistic language without proposing a specific mechanism — "the government should raise awareness," "people should be more responsible," or "authorities need to take action." These phrases sound reasonable but give the examiner nothing concrete to credit as developed Task Response.

**Vague (Band 6 pattern):**

> The government should do more to solve traffic problems and people should try to use public transport more.

**Specific (Band 7+ pattern):**

> City authorities could expand bus rapid transit routes into underserved suburbs and introduce congestion pricing during peak hours, directly reducing the number of drivers who currently have no practical alternative to a private car.

The specific version names an actor, a mechanism, and a result — the three elements a vague solution usually skips.

## Quick self-check before you submit

Run through these checks with about two minutes remaining:

1. **List your causes and solutions side by side** on scrap paper or mentally — does each solution name an actor (who), a mechanism (how), and a result (what changes)?
2. **Check the direction of your body 1.** If the prompt asked for "problems caused by X," confirm you wrote effects, not causes — and the reverse if it asked for "causes of X."
3. **Read your body 2 opening sentence alone.** Could it apply to almost any unrelated problem? If so, it likely needs a specific mechanism added.
4. **Confirm your conclusion references both halves** — the problem and the proposed fix — rather than only restating the topic.

## Problem/solution vs advantages/disadvantages

These two types are sometimes confused because both discuss a negative and a positive angle on a topic. The distinction is what each half actually represents: advantages/disadvantages weighs the pros and cons of something that already exists or is proposed (should it happen at all?), while problem/solution assumes the problem is real and asks how to fix it (given that it exists, what should be done?). A prompt about "the rise of private car use" could go either way depending on the exact question — check whether it asks you to weigh the trend itself or to solve a problem it causes. See [advantage disadvantage essay: 4-paragraph formula](/blog/advantage-disadvantage-4-paragraph) for the full contrast.

## Try this yourself

**Prompt:** Obesity rates are rising in many countries. What are the causes of this trend, and what solutions can you suggest?

1. Write two causes as complete sentences, each naming a mechanism (not just "unhealthy food").
2. Write one solution for each cause, checking that it responds to that specific cause rather than a general idea.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between "causes" and "problems" in this essay type?

"Causes" asks why something happens (the reasons behind it); "problems" asks what negative effects it produces. Reading the exact prompt wording tells you which direction body 1 should take before you start planning solutions.

### How many causes and solutions should I include?

One or two of each, fully developed with a mechanism and example, scores higher than three or four causes listed without explanation. Depth beats a longer list.

### Do my solutions have to match my causes exactly?

Yes, ideally in the same order. A body 2 that introduces unrelated ideas rather than fixes for body 1's specific causes tends to read as two disconnected paragraphs.

### What makes a solution "vague" in IELTS terms?

A vague solution suggests a general intention ("raise awareness," "take action") without naming who does what, or how it addresses the specific cause described earlier.

### Is this the same as an advantages and disadvantages essay?

No. Problem/solution focuses on the causes of one issue and matching fixes; advantages/disadvantages weighs the pros and cons of a trend or practice. See [advantage disadvantage essay: 4-paragraph formula](/blog/advantage-disadvantage-4-paragraph) for the contrast.
